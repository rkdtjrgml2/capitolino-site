You Can't Predict. You Can Prepare.

Capitolino · Aug 28, 2026

Everyone in the market this week is waiting for the same sentence.

Kevin Warsh takes the podium at Jackson Hole, his first as Fed chair. September 16 is three weeks away. Inflation is still above 2%. July payrolls just went negative. And a lot of smart people have reduced that entire picture to one bet: hike, or not.

That is the wrong question. The useful ones are older than this cycle.

1. “You can’t predict. You can prepare.” — Howard Marks

The Federal Open Market Committee held the funds rate at 3.50–3.75% in July, 9–3. Three officials wanted a hike on the spot. Since then the tape has pulled in two directions at once.

July CPI eased to 3.4% from 3.5%. Core is near 2.5%, the lowest in months. Then the jobs report: minus 23,000 payrolls, and the prior two months revised down by about 100,000. September hike odds, which were around 60% after the meeting, have fallen to roughly one in three.

So the crowd is trying to predict the next 25 basis points. Marks’s line is the correction. Prediction is a single path. Preparation is owning the fact that both of these can stay true at the same time: inflation has not returned to 2%, and the labor market is no longer doing the Fed any favors.

A 9–3 vote is not a forecast. It is a split mechanism. Prepare for a chair who may not resolve it in one speech.

2. “Never, ever invest in the present.” — Stanley Druckenmiller

Druckenmiller’s point is mechanical, not mystical. The present is already in the price. The speech this morning, the 34% hike probability, the Mag 7 still around a third of the S&P 500 — that is the present.

What is not fully priced is the collision underneath it. Sticky prices on one side of the mandate. A labor market that just printed its first outright payroll decline in months on the other. A Treasury that has already stepped into long bonds to hold yields down. A chair who has spent his first months refusing to give the market a reaction function, and telling it to read market signals instead.

If you buy or sell the speech, you are investing in the present. The trade that matters is what the next two prints do to that collision: August jobs on September 4, August CPI on September 11, then the meeting on the 16th.

3. “When the facts change, I change my mind.” — attributed to Keynes

Hike odds did not drift from 60% to 35% because people became wiser. They moved because one jobs report changed the fact set.

That is the whole lesson. The dangerous habit in a week like this is to marry the last narrative. Last month the narrative was “inflation is still too high, they have to hike.” This month it is “the labor market is cracking, they cannot hike.” Both were inferences from real numbers. Neither is a personality.

Keynes’s line, whether he said it exactly that way or not, is the operating rule: the fact is the print, not the story you told about the last print.

What this is not

This is not “don’t fight the Fed.” That proverb assumes a Fed that agrees with itself. This one does not. Three dissenters wanted a hike in July. The new chair will not tell you September in advance. Fighting “the Fed” when the Fed is an argument is just picking a side of a 9–3 vote and calling it wisdom.

And it is not a crash call. Concentration in seven names is high. Valuations are not 1999. Payrolls went negative once. One print is a fact. A trend is several.

The thing to keep in your pocket

When the tape is waiting on one man in Wyoming, pull Marks out of your pocket.

You cannot know what Warsh will say, and you cannot know what September will do. You can know the mechanism: two mandates pulling opposite ways, a committee that already showed you the split, and a calendar that will settle it with data, not with a keynote.

Prepare for both. Do not buy the present. Change your mind when the next print actually changes the facts.
